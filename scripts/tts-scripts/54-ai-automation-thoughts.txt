Listened to a long podcast today about the economic effects of automation and language models and came away with a more nuanced view than I started with. The framing I'd internalized, that AI will mostly automate repetitive tasks and free humans for creative work, was challenged by several examples where the creative and judgment-intensive work is actually more automatable than the physical dexterous work that requires human presence. Plumbers and electricians may be more durable than knowledge workers in the near term, which is counterintuitive given the historical relationship between education and economic security. What landed for me was the argument that the question isn't whether your job disappears but whether the skills that make you valuable are ones that compound with AI tools rather than compete against them. Understanding what the tools can do, being able to direct and evaluate their output, staying on top of what's possible, these seem like durable advantages. I've been using language models heavily in my work for about a year now and the productivity difference on certain tasks is so substantial that I find it hard to imagine going back. But I'm also aware that being a heavy user of a tool is different from understanding its limits and failure modes, which requires more intentional study. Going to spend some time this month deliberately exploring where these systems break down rather than just using them where they work.
