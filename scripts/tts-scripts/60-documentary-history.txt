Watched a documentary series on the Byzantine Empire last night and ended up going down a rabbit hole until one in the morning. The thing that keeps striking me about Byzantine history is how contingent everything was. An empire that lasted over a thousand years and produced one of the most sophisticated cultures of the medieval world came within a hair of collapsing dozens of times before it finally fell in 1453. The siege of Constantinople by the Umayyad Caliphate in the early eighth century, which most people have never heard of, was arguably as consequential as any battle in Western European history, and the Greek fire that repelled it kept an entire civilization intact. What I find endlessly fascinating about history is exactly this, not the great inevitable movements but the moments of contingency where things could easily have gone differently, where one decision or one piece of technology or one person's illness changed everything downstream. The documentary did a good job of putting the political history into cultural context, the theological controversies, the extraordinary architecture, the preservation of classical Greek knowledge that later fed the Italian Renaissance. History taught in school is so often stripped of this texture that it stops being interesting. Going to read Judith Herrin's book on Byzantium that the documentary mentioned as a follow-up. Already ordered it.
